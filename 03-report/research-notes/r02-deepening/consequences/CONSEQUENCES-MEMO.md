# R02 Concrete consequences and operating resilience

28 October 2026. Internal desktop evidence assessment. Two primary employer records and one regulator bulletin supplement the existing research. No participant contact, private account access, forms, purchases or canonical-record edits occurred.

## Conclusion

The evidence supplies concrete mechanisms rather than a typical payroll-error cost. Consequences include supplementary payment and recovery work, reconciliation and reporting discrepancies, disruption to employees, and additional control activity. A service can maintain payments through substantial human effort while still having material weaknesses. Headline service continuity therefore does not establish spare capacity or a fully effective process.

Treat these as bounded examples. Both new employer cases are local authorities, selected because their evidence is public; they are not representative of private employers. The bulletin describes a specific reporting mechanism without an incidence estimate. No payroll-only monetary cost of administrative rework was established.

## Case 1 Oldham Council

[Payroll Audit Progress](https://committees.oldham.gov.uk/documents/s155079/Payroll%20Audit%20Progress.pdf), 22 October 2025, printed pp1–5, especially sections 2.3–2.6, 3.3–3.10 and 4.6.

The council's management report describes a service covering 27 monthly payrolls and almost 8,500 staff. It records 153 underpayments in 2024/25 and says recorded underpayments were rectified through supplementary payment routes. It also records overpayments requiring recovery action. The report explicitly acknowledges incomplete systematic error classification. Its staffing, knowledge and capacity explanation is management attribution.

The launched improvement activity includes a cross-functional reconciliation group and tighter payment approval; system optimisation and further capacity gains are planned rather than demonstrated benefits. This is useful evidence of correction/control workload and the need for records. Do not divide incident counts by an illustrative transaction count from a different period or call the reported actions proof of a completed turnaround.

## Case 2 Comhairle nan Eilean Siar

[Payroll and Expenses internal audit](https://www.cne-siar.gov.uk/sites/default/files/imce/Committees/Audit%20and%20Scrutiny%20Committee/2025/June/O-15A-Final-Report-Payroll-and-Expenses.pdf), final issue 23 May 2025, printed pp2–3 and findings M2–M6, pp7–10. Visits were February/March 2025; the cover and issue log say final although body headers retain draft wording.

The audit links late/incomplete departmental forms to payment problems and additional recovery work. It records £106,529.57 of advances affecting 142 employees in December 2023–December 2024. That is remedial payment value, not an administrative cost or loss. Separate overpayment totals are gross amounts, with recoveries at different stages; do not sum them with advances or present them as write-offs.

The audit gave Reasonable Assurance and praised maintained service after a cyberattack, while documenting control weaknesses and capacity pressure. Selected uploads also had no issues. This balance matters: successful payment continuity and improvement needs can coexist. The local £50 internal charging policy mentioned in M4 was not being applied and is not an observed unit cost of correcting an error.

## Mechanism 3 HMRC reporting identity errors

[April 2026 Employer Bulletin](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/employer-bulletin-april-2026/april-2026-issue-of-the-employer-bulletin), published 15 April 2026, section RTI submission problems and incorrect handling of payroll ID.

HMRC describes how incorrectly handled payroll IDs can create duplicate employment records, wrong year-to-date information, repeated employer contact and manual correction. Reused IDs can attach data to the wrong record and create disputed charges. This is primary operational guidance and a reported mechanism, not measured prevalence or a quantified loss study. The outcome needing protection includes correct statutory records as well as the payment itself. Detailed current correction instructions belong in the official guidance rather than JGA's report.

## What to do with this evidence

For a payroll leader, ask which task produces the additional work, whether its owner is inside or outside payroll, and what currently prevents recurrence. For a supplier, clarify whether support should concern input validation, approval visibility, correction workflow, reconciliations or operating support. These are conditional interpretations, not findings that a named product is absent or that software can fix every issue.

For JGA's original research, distinguish four outcomes: correct/timely payment, correct statutory reporting, reliable accounting/control evidence, and understandable employee service. A case can perform well on one and poorly on another. The existing core and selective probes can capture this without another survey. Track actual observed results separately from management plans and participant beliefs.

## Monetary handling

- Gross overpayments are not necessarily irrecoverable loss. Keep recoveries, amounts outstanding, provisions and write-offs separate if available.
- Advances and supplementary payments are usually remedial cash movements; do not treat the principal as avoidable administrative cost.
- A local charging policy is not a measured resource cost or a transferable benchmark.
- No quantified retention effect, trust score, error cost per employee or payroll-specific programme ROI was established here.
- Avoid attributing combined ERP/HR/finance programme budgets to payroll alone. Existing Scottish Government and NAO cases remain contextual and are not re-counted as fresh cases.

## Counterevidence and remaining gaps

Comhairle's assurance and continuity finding and its selected no-issue samples challenge a universal failure story. Oldham's corrective actions demonstrate a response, but not yet its measured net benefit. HMRC also describes corrective mechanisms; identification of a failure mode is not evidence that every employer experiences it.

The most useful original contribution will be the chain from need to consequence, response, owner and observed result in the actual JGA cohort. We still need permissioned cases, realistic baseline/recurrence evidence and differences between a temporary disruption and a persistent service shortfall. Public reporting is more available for public bodies, so this evidence cannot establish private-market demand.
