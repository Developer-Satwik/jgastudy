# R04 second challenge: authority changes over time

29 October 2026. Internal desktop challenge. No original practitioner evidence, policy-compliance advice or private-employer prevalence claim.

## What this adds

The first pass correctly dates decisions and separates functions. Add an explicit question about whether the authority arrangement was effective for that event, and whether it changed. A title can stay constant while delegated remit, central involvement, contract scope or escalation conditions change. An approval inherited from an earlier stage is not automatically authority for a later, materially different decision.

Three inspected primary HTML routes provide a bounded historical/current contrast. They are not a before-and-after performance study and do not establish a general trend towards centralisation or decentralisation across payroll employers.

## Two refinements to the analytical design

**Effective authority and change:** record the event's relevant period, described mandate/approval route, any known change and its basis. Distinguish an intended revised route from one actually used. Approximate or unknown dates remain valid; no confidential delegation letter is required.

**Organisational and contractual layer:** identify whose decision it was and on whose behalf the function could act: for example, central/group, local entity or service/customer relationship. Record the affected scope and actual escalation trigger. A framework role, service-owner role, customer contract and group title need not confer the same rights. These examples are analytical possibilities, not a required corporate hierarchy.

Use selective clarification under Q001/Q006–Q009/Q013. Do not add mandatory questions or make a complete historical governance reconstruction an eligibility requirement. Preserve the directly experienced account before showing any role list or constructed prior-study scenario.

## Counterevidence and stopping rule

More central sign-offs are not presumed better. The 2026 material supplies a counterpoint to an assumption that strong governance requires repeated central approvals; assurance can remain while approval responsibility changes. Conversely, delegation does not mean absence of controls or an entitlement to ignore unresolved conditions. A central mandate and local acceptance may coexist, but the actual case must establish the arrangement.

The published Scottish positive governance assessment and the first-pass Rotherham continuity decision remain in the synthesis. This pass does not replace them with a failure narrative. It also does not infer that the 2016 structures persisted through 2026 or that later policy resolved the older programme's problems.
