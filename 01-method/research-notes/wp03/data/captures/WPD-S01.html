Does an organisation always need my consent to use my personal information?

Click to toggle details
Latest updates - 23 October 2026

23 October 2026 - this guidance was updated following amendments to the Data (Use and Access) Act. It also includes new examples and a template letter that can be used to send to organisation to withdraw consent. 

No. Organisations often won’t need your consent to use your personal information. They can use it without your consent if they have another valid reason. This is called having a ‘lawful basis’.

What are the lawful bases?

There are seven lawful bases and an organisation needs to apply at least one of these to use your personal information. They are:

  • consent;
  • contract;
  • legal obligation;
  • vital interests;
  • public task;
  • recognised legitimate interest; and
  • legitimate interests.

Consent is the most well-known, but they're all equally important. The organisation must choose the most appropriate basis. This depends on what they want to do with your information.

Most of the lawful bases depend on the use of your personal information being necessary and proportionate for a particular purpose.

Further reading – ICO guidance

See our guide to lawful basis and our advice about when using your personal information is necessary. We made this for organisations but you may find it helpful.

When can an organisation rely on consent?

Consent is appropriate if the organisation can offer you a real choice and control over how they use your personal information. If an organisation decides that this is the most appropriate lawful basis, they need to make sure that:

  • your consent is freely given, meaning that you can say no and it won't unfairly disadvantage you;
  • they ask for your consent through a clear positive action, like ticking a box, rather than assuming your consent;
  • the request for your consent is clear, easy to understand and separate from other things, such as terms and conditions;
  • the organisation clearly explains what you're consenting to and if any third parties will also rely on your consent; and
  • you can withdraw your consent at any time, in a way that's just as easy as giving it.

Example

An organisation asks if you'd like to receive a newsletter by email from their partner company. You decide that you do, so you tick the relevant box on their online form to provide your consent.

Is consenting the same as agreeing to something?

Not necessarily. Consent in data protection law has a different meaning to ‘consent’ in everyday use. Often organisations have other reasons they ask for your ‘consent’, or permission, which are unrelated to data protection consent.

For example, you may agree to sign a contract or agree to terms and conditions. However, this doesn't mean the organisation is using consent as their lawful basis.

Example

Your GP tells you they want to refer you to a specialist. To not breach confidentiality, they ask for your consent to share medical records. This is due to rules within the health sector and makes sure you're involved in decisions about your care.

Asking for your permission or agreement may not mean your GP is also relying on consent as the lawful basis.

Consent is rarely the most appropriate lawful basis for a medical practice. In this situation, public task or legitimate interests may be more appropriate.

When can an organisation rely on children’s consent?

Children have the same rights as adults over their personal information. They can provide consent themselves, as long as they can understand what they're consenting to and it's clear they're not acting against their own best interests.

This means that when relying on consent, organisations must make sure the information they provide is easy to understand and suitable for the child's age.

In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, there's no set minimum age at which a child can give their own consent. What matters is whether they can understand what they're agreeing to. In Scotland, children are generally presumed to have this understanding from the age of 12, so this is often a useful guide.

If a child doesn't have sufficient understanding but the organisation still wants to rely on consent as the lawful basis, they usually need consent from someone with parental responsibility. This may not be appropriate if involving that person would be against the child's best interests, for example where a service is providing confidential counselling or support.

There are specific rules for online services that are offered directly to children, if they're relying on consent. Children aged 13 and over can lawfully give their own consent. However, for children under 13 organisations must get and reasonably verify parental consent.

Does a school need to rely on consent to share my child’s personal information?

There are many reasons it might be necessary for a school to share information about your child to a partner agency, such as:

  • to report attendance data to the local authority;
  • for safeguarding purposes; or
  • to help the child receive special educational needs support.

Schools don't need to rely on consent as the lawful basis when sharing personal information for safeguarding purposes or when carrying out their normal statutory duties.

There's likely to be a different, more appropriate lawful basis than consent for schools to rely on, particularly as there's often an imbalance of power between children and schools.

In this case, a state-funded school might rely on public task. A private school may need to rely on a different lawful basis, such as legitimate interests or legal obligation. The school must decide the most appropriate lawful basis.

Does an organisation need my consent to send me direct marketing?

Not necessarily. Organisations may ask for your consent to send you marketing but they don't always need to. They may seek to rely on the legitimate interests lawful basis instead. The most appropriate lawful basis will depend on the situation.

There are additional privacy laws, known as the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations (PECR), which apply to electronic direct marketing and require consent. These rules say that organisations may need your consent to send you electronic marketing, but there are some exceptions. If consent is required under PECR, then the appropriate lawful basis under data protection law should also be consent.

Find out more about marketing emails and marketing texts on our website.

Example

An organisation wants to tell existing customers about similar products to those they've already bought. They decide that postal marketing is an effective way to do this and consider that existing customers are probably happy to receive this. They also consider that customers are likely to reasonably expect to receive such marketing, as there's already an existing relationship.

The organisation could rely on consent, for example by asking customers to opt in when they make a purchase. However, they decide to rely on legitimate interests and to offer an opt-out option when someone makes a purchase. The organisation asks customers to confirm if they don't want to receive marketing letters and includes a way to opt out in every message after that.

Further reading – ICO guidance

Our sending direct marketing guidance for organisations has more detail about choosing a lawful basis for different methods of direct marketing.

How do I withdraw my consent?

If an organisation is relying on consent as their lawful basis for using your personal information, you have the right to withdraw that consent at any time. This doesn't affect the use of your personal information up to that point, but it does mean the organisation must stop using any information that was based on your consent. They should stop as soon as possible, although in some cases there might be a short delay.

This only applies when you've provided an organisation with consent to use your personal information. If the organisation is relying on a different lawful basis, then you can't withdraw your consent. However, you may have the right to object to the organisation's use of your personal information. You can read our right to object guidance for more information.

Organisations should explain how you can withdraw your consent in their privacy information. For example, they may have an online form you can use to withdraw your consent. You could also contact the organisation in writing, for example by email. If you're not sure how to get in touch with an organisation, you should be able to find their contact details in their privacy notice.

There are no specific words you must use to withdraw your consent, but you may find the template below helpful.

Template letter

[Name of the organisation]
[Address of the organisation (if known)]
[Reference number (if applicable)]

Dear [name of team / department / the person you've been in contact with]

Right to withdraw my consent

[Your name and any other details to help identify you]

I'd like to exercise my right under data protection law to withdraw my consent to the use of my personal information.

[Give details of what you're withdrawing your consent to]

You can find guidance on your obligations under information rights legislation on the Information Commission's website.

Please send a full response as soon as possible.

If there's anything you'd like to discuss, please contact me on [provide preferred contact method].

Yours sincerely

[Signature]

What is the contract lawful basis?

An organisation may rely on the contract lawful basis when it's necessary to use your personal information because:

  • you have a contract with them; or
  • you've asked them to take certain steps before you enter into a contract with them, like providing you with a quote.

Example

You ask a car insurance company for a quote. To do this, they use your personal information, such as your contact details and claims history.

They can rely on contract as their lawful basis because you've asked them to take steps towards a contract, even though you haven't agreed to anything yet.

You decide to take out the insurance, so the company continues to use your personal information to provide the policy. At this point, there's a contract in place, so they still rely on the contract lawful basis.

The age at which someone is considered to have the required level of understanding to enter into a contract differs across the UK. In Scotland it's generally 16, while in the rest of the UK it's generally 18. There can be exceptions to this, for example if it's necessary for a person under 18 to enter a contract to obtain shelter or food.

What is the legal obligation lawful basis?

An organisation may rely on the legal obligation lawful basis when it's necessary to use your personal information to comply with the law or regulatory requirement, for example a law outside of data protection.

Example

Your employer processes your personal information because it's necessary to comply with their legal obligation to disclose employee salary details to HMRC.

What is the vital interests lawful basis?

An organisation may rely on the vital interests lawful basis when it's necessary to use or share your personal information to protect either your life or another person's life in an emergency.

It's most likely to apply to your health information which is needed at that time. It's not normally appropriate for pre-planned medical care.

Example

You become unconscious following an incident at work. Your employer provides the ambulance crew with information about you and your health. Because you can't provide these details yourself and it's an emergency, they rely on vital interests as the lawful basis for sharing your information.

What is the public task lawful basis?

An organisation may rely on the public task lawful basis if it's necessary to use personal information to carry out their official role or a specific task in the public interest.

It's mainly relevant to public sector organisations, such as certain schools, councils, emergency services, government departments or health and social care organisations.

Example

Your employer shares your personal information with HMRC so they can calculate the right amount of tax. As HMRC is required to use your personal information for this purpose, they can rely on the public task lawful basis.

Example

The Department for Work and Pensions (DWP) contacts you to conduct a review of your universal credit allowance. They ask for copies of your personal information, such as your bank statement. Because the DWP have an obligation in law to carry out claim reviews, they rely on public task as their lawful basis.

Further reading – ICO guidance

You have the right to object when an organisation relies on the public task lawful basis.

What is the recognised legitimate interest lawful basis?

An organisation may rely on the recognised legitimate interest lawful basis if it's necessary to use your personal information for one of the pre-approved purposes listed below, which are set out in law and are recognised as being in the public interest.

An organisation might rely on this basis to use or share your personal information for:

  • crime prevention;
  • public security, national security or defence;
  • safeguarding;
  • emergencies; or
  • other organisations to perform their public task or official functions.

The organisation must tell you which of these apply.

Further reading – ICO guidance

You have the right to object when an organisation relies on the recognised legitimate interest lawful basis.

See our Recognised legitimate interest guidance for more detail. We made this for organisations but you may find it useful.

What is the legitimate interests lawful basis?

An organisation may rely on the legitimate interests lawful basis if it's necessary to use your personal information for their own legitimate interests or those of a third party. It's likely to be appropriate when an organisation wants to use your personal information in ways you'd expect and that have minimal impact on your privacy or if the organisation has a compelling reason for using your personal information. It covers a wide range of situations.

The organisation also needs to balance the reason they want to use your personal information against your interests, rights and freedoms. The greater the impact on you, the stronger the organisation's justification needs to be. If your rights and freedoms override the organisation's interests, then they cannot rely on this lawful basis.

Organisations can rely on the legitimate interests basis to use children's information, but they have to take extra care to protect their interests. If there's a serious mismatch between an organisation's interests and the interests, rights and freedoms of the child, the organisation should assume the child's interests are stronger and put them first.

An organisation must tell you what their legitimate interests are.

Example

Your sister is asked by her employer for the contact details of a relative in case she has an accident or becomes seriously ill at work. She gives her employer your contact details because she wants you to be her emergency contact.

Her employer considers that being able to contact you in an emergency is a legitimate interest as a responsible employer. It's also in the interests of both you and your sister that you're told about the emergency.

As the employer will only access your contact details in an actual emergency and the impact of holding those details is very low, they decide that they can rely on legitimate interests to process your details.

Example

An organisation shares a person's information with a debt collection agency to recover money owed for services they provided.

The organisation has a legitimate purpose, which is to recover the unpaid debt. They decide that passing on the person's information to a debt collection agency is necessary.

The organisation is satisfied that the person's interests, rights and freedoms don't outweigh their need to recover the debt.

They only share information about the debt and they take into account that most people would expect an organisation to take steps to recover an unpaid debt.

In this case, the organisation may rely on legitimate interests to share personal information about the debt. They could also rely on contract, where recovering payment is necessary to perform or enforce a contract they hold with that person.

The debt collection agency also has a legitimate interest as a business in recovering the debt. They may also rely on legitimate interests to process the person's information.

If the organisation was a public authority, such as a council recovering a council tax bill, they may rely on public task or legal obligation to share the information. This is because legitimate interests doesn't apply to a public authority carrying out their official tasks.

Further reading – ICO guidance

You have the right to object when an organisation relies on the legitimate interests lawful basis.

What can I expect from the organisation?

An organisation needs to tell you why they want to use your personal information and what their lawful basis is. They need to decide on the lawful basis before they use your personal information. Generally, they can't swap from one lawful basis to another.

The organisation needs to proactively provide you with this information in their privacy information. It should be on their website, but if not you could ask them to send it to you. Their privacy information needs to tell you:

  • why they're using your personal information; and
  • which of the seven lawful bases they're relying on to use your personal information.

Organisations often use your personal information for a variety of purposes, so they may be relying on more than one lawful basis.

What should I do if I’m unhappy with how an organisation has used my personal information?

If you think an organisation hasn't followed data protection law when using your personal information, you can complain to them. Data protection law requires organisations to have a process to handle complaints.

You can also complain to us. However, we recommend giving the organisation a chance to complete their complaint handling process first. This gives them the opportunity to put things right and may mean we can handle your complaint more quickly if you're unhappy with the organisation's response.
